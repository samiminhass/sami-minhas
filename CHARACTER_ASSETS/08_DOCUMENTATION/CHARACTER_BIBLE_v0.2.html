1. World Concept

Millbrook — A Village Between Seasons

A small, self-contained village nestled in a valley between low, rounded hills and dense forests. The architecture is hand-built: thick stone walls, timber frames, clay-tile roofs weathered to irregular earthy tones. Streets are narrow, paved in uneven cobblestone. Every surface carries the patina of generations—worn doorsteps, faded painted signs, iron hinges green with age.

Key Locations

The Bakery
Heart of the village. Warm light spills from small windows. Flour dust hangs in the air. The oven is enormous and ancient, built into the stone wall itself.
The Square
A small irregular plaza with a stone fountain (dry in summer, overflowing in spring). Market stalls appear on certain days. Pigeons and stray cats.
The Old Bridge
A single stone arch over a narrow stream. Moss-covered, slightly crooked. The place where children gather and secrets are told.
The Meadow
Beyond the last houses. Wildflowers in every season. Rolling hills, scattered trees, a view of distant mountains. Where the girl goes to think.
The Workshop Shed
A leaning timber outbuilding behind someone's garden. Full of junk, gears, half-built contraptions, and the smell of machine oil and sawdust.
The Grandmother's Cottage
Slightly apart from the village, surrounded by an overgrown garden that seems to bloom regardless of season. Herbs hang from the ceiling inside.
The Forest Edge
Where the known world ends. Dense, old-growth trees. Strange sounds. Paths that seem to shift. Nobody goes far in.
The Cellar Market
Beneath the square. Vaulted stone ceilings, lantern-lit. Where unusual things are traded, and rumors begin.

World Rules

  • The world is grounded and believable. Physics work. People get tired, hungry, wet.
  • Subtle strangeness exists at the edges: a door that wasn't there yesterday, a flower that blooms in frost, a sound from the forest with no source.
  • Magic is never explained or systematic. It is atmospheric, ambiguous, and rare.
  • Seasons change and matter. Winter means real cold. Summer means real heat. The village transforms with each season.
  • Animals behave like animals. They are not comic sidekicks. (A cat is a cat.)
  • The village has a past. There are things the adults don't talk about. Old photographs. A locked room. An abandoned building.

2. Art Direction

Visual Identity

Handcrafted stop-motion-inspired cinematic 3D. Every surface should look like it was made by hand—stitched, carved, sculpted, knitted, baked. The world should feel like a physical miniature set brought to life, with the precision and warmth of Laika-quality production design, but with our own proportional language, color identity, and character philosophy.

Lighting Philosophy

  • Exterior: Warm golden-hour key, cool sky fill, subtle rim separation. Time-of-day shifts between scenes.
  • Interior (bakery): Warm practical lights (oven glow, window light), deep shadows, orange fill from fire.
  • Interior (cottage): Dappled light through small windows, filtered through plants and curtains. Soft, green-tinged.
  • Night: Deep blue environment, practical warm lamplight, strong contrast.

Proportional Rules (All Characters)

  • Heads are approximately 1/3 to 1/4 of total body height (large, expressive).
  • Hands are slightly oversized for emotional gesturing.
  • Feet are grounded and substantial—big enough to convey weight.
  • Eyes are the dominant facial feature. Eye size scales with emotional openness of the character.
  • Proportions express personality: round = warm/safe, angular = sharp/guarded, tall/thin = nervous/energetic.

Vertical & Mobile Readability

Our first releases are 15–45 second vertical shorts watched on phones. Every character design must hold up at that size.

  • Expressions at phone size: each of the eight expressions must be identifiable in a 9:16 medium shot viewed at 360 × 640 px. If a tester can't name the emotion in about one second, the expression needs a stronger shape change, not more detail.
  • Eye contrast: eyes need a clear value step between white, iris and skin, plus a dependable catchlight. In short-form, the eyes carry most reactions.
  • Silhouette in a tall frame: each signature shape (the Girl's braid and bunny, the Baker's width and mustache) must read when the character fills about half the frame height.
  • Two-shots: 9:16 leaves little width. The Girl–Baker size ratio should support staging in depth, with one character nearer the camera, instead of side by side. We will check this with a blockout two-shot before locking proportions.
  • Dark values: the Girl's black hair and the Baker's dark mustache and brows need rim or edge separation in every lighting setup. Without it they merge into dark backgrounds and lose shape in compressed mobile video.

Known risk: the Baker's eyes. In the reference they are small and shadowed by his brows. That suits his personality but may disappear at phone size. Proposed handling, without redesigning him: keep the eye size, add a dependable catchlight, and give the brows enough lift to reveal the eyes on key beats. The mustache and brows stay his main expression carriers. We will test this at blockout and report back before changing anything visible.

3. Character 01 — The Girl Approved Reference

A. Identity

Category
Protagonist / Lead
Age Impression
8–10 years old
Role
Central character. The audience's entry point into Millbrook.
Emotional Core
Fierce tenderness. She protects what she loves with everything she has, even when she's scared.
Personality
Brave, curious, stubborn, imaginative, private, loyal
Greatest Strength
She never abandons someone or something she cares about.
Main Weakness
She doesn't easily trust new people. She holds on too tight—to her bunny, to her routines, to the way things were.
Core Contradiction
She craves adventure but is afraid of losing what she already has.
What Makes Her Interesting
She looks like she's been through something. There's a visible history in her worn clothes, her fierce grip on the bunny, her wariness. But she hasn't become hard—she's still open enough to be surprised.

B. Naming Proposals

Mila Recommended
Slavic origin, means "gracious" or "dear." Short, warm, easy to say in any language. Feels like a real village child's name, not a fantasy invention. The soft "m" opening and the open "a" ending carry warmth, but the name is also crisp enough for a character who can be sharp. Works naturally in dialogue: "Mila, come inside." "Mila found it." "That's Mila's."
Luma
Latin root for "light." Slightly more unusual, slightly more lyrical. Evokes the warm glow of the village. Could feel too precious if the character needs to also feel scrappy and rough.
Wren
English, after the small determined bird. Strong consonant, one syllable, feels gutsy and compact. Matches her scrappy energy. Risk: might feel more Anglo/modern than the European village setting.
Runa
Norse origin, meaning "secret" or "hidden lore." Connects to the mysterious edges of the world. A name that carries story within it—why was she named this?

C. Physical Design (From Approved Reference)

Height
~4 heads tall. Compact, sturdy build.
Head
Large and round. Wide forehead. Strong chin for a child. Head-to-body ratio emphasizes vulnerability and expressiveness.
Hair
Black, messy, wild flyaways. One very long braid wrapped in crisscrossed cord/twine, reaching past her waist. The braid is heavy—a physical weight she carries.
Eyes
Large, dark brown, wide-set. Dominant facial feature. Slightly upturned outer corners. High, expressive eyebrows.
Nose
Small, rounded, slightly upturned. Freckles across the bridge and cheeks.
Mouth
Small, slightly asymmetric natural resting expression—not quite a smile, not neutral. Capable of very wide expressions.
Skin
Warm peach-tan with visible freckles. Subtle subsurface warmth. Slightly ruddy cheeks and nose tip.
Torso
Coral/salmon loose-fitting tunic over cream long sleeves. Tunic is faded, slightly stained. Hem is uneven.
Lower
Patched denim capri-length jeans. Visible hand-stitched repair patches in lighter denim.
Feet
Red canvas sneakers, well-worn, slightly scuffed. Thick rubber soles. The one modern-feeling element, grounding her in relatability.
Signature Object
Handmade stuffed bunny rabbit. Cream/tan fabric, simple stitched face, floppy ears, well-loved and slightly misshapen from use. She carries it cradled in both arms or tucked under one arm.

4. Character 02 — The Baker Approved Reference

A. Identity

Category
Major Supporting / Potential Deuteragonist
Age Impression
50–60 years old
Role
The village's anchor. His bakery is where people come, whether they need bread or not. His relationship with the girl is the emotional spine of the series.
Emotional Core
A man who lost something important and replaced the absence with routine, discipline, and bread. His gruffness is armor. The girl is slowly finding a crack in it.
Personality
Gruff, proud, territorial, ritualistic, surprisingly perceptive, quietly generous
Greatest Strength
He makes everyone feel fed—literally and emotionally—without admitting he cares.
Main Weakness
He can't say what he feels. He substitutes action for words, bread for affection, grumbling for concern.
Core Contradiction
He pushes people away with his manner and draws them in with his actions.
What Makes Him Interesting
His design is comic (the mustache, the belly, the arms-crossed stance) but his emotional truth is deeply sympathetic. He is simultaneously the funniest and the saddest character.

B. Naming Proposals

Borek Recommended
Central/Eastern European feel. The hard "B" and the rolling "r" match his physical presence. Also subtly evokes "borek," a baked pastry—a quiet joke embedded in the name. Sounds like someone who would fold his arms and say nothing. Easy for children to say and remember.
Gustl
Bavarian diminutive of Gustav. Affectionate despite the character's gruffness—suggests someone the village has known since he was a boy. Warm, familiar, slightly comic.
Hamish
Scottish Gaelic form of James. Sounds sturdy, heavy, dependable. Matches the barrel-shaped physique. Risk: might locate the world too specifically in Scotland.
Tadek
Polish diminutive of Tadeusz. Warm, compact, grandfatherly. Has a gentle ending that contrasts with his tough exterior.

C. Physical Design (From Approved Reference)

Height
~3.5 heads tall. Extremely stocky. Wide as he is tall.
Build
Barrel-shaped torso, massive forearms, short thick legs. His mass is in his upper body. Center of gravity is high and forward.
Head
Large, round, sits directly on shoulders with almost no visible neck. Wide jaw.
Hair
Dark brown, thinning, messy on top. Almost bald. Wild bushy eyebrows that extend past the eye socket.
Eyes
Small, deep-set, partially hidden by the enormous eyebrows. Dark. Suspicious and observant.
Nose
Bulbous, prominent, pinkish-red. The warmest-colored point on his face. Suggests both temperament and temperature (the oven).
Mouth
Hidden behind massive curled handlebar mustache. Mouth movements must be readable through the mustache shape and cheek movement.
Facial Hair
Signature feature: thick, dark brown handlebar mustache, waxed into dramatic upward curls. The mustache is his pride. It moves independently as a secondary animation feature.
Torso
Cream/off-white baker's jacket. Flour-dusted. Wooden buttons. Slightly too tight across the belly. Linen apron tied over it, stained with flour and dough.
Lower
Burgundy/maroon loose trousers gathered at the knee. Thick, utilitarian.
Feet
Wooden clogs. Simple, practical, loud on cobblestone. Flour-dusted.
Arms
Forearms are exposed and hairy. Thick wrists. Hands are enormous—kneading hands. Often crossed over his chest.
Signature Object
A round bread loaf, often carried under one arm or held out to someone (the only way he says "I care").

Character Colors

D. Animation Identity

Posture
Arms crossed, chest out, chin slightly raised. Default stance says "go away."
Walk
Heavy, deliberate, slightly waddling. Clogs clop on stone. The ground feels him coming.
Work
When baking, he transforms. His movements become precise, rhythmic, graceful. Kneading is a dance. This contrast reveals his inner life.
Idle
Adjusts apron. Wipes hands on cloth. Sniffs the air (checking the oven). Strokes mustache.
Signature Gesture
The bread hand-off: he shoves a loaf toward someone without looking at them, as if he's annoyed by his own generosity.
Emotional Tell
His mustache droops when he's sad and bristles upward when he's proud or pleased. It's involuntary.

E. Required Expressions

ExpressionKey Features
NeutralArms crossed. Brow furrowed. Mustache level. Eyes half-lidded. Skeptical.
HappyHe won't smile outright. The mustache lifts at the corners. Eyes soften. Chin drops slightly.
SadMustache droops. Eyebrows lose their furrow. He looks smaller. Shoulders drop.
AngryFull bristle. Mustache points straight out. Face reddens. Nostrils flare. Hands become fists.
SurprisedEyebrows shoot above the eye line (visible for once). Mustache splays. Mouth opens behind it.
WorriedOne hand goes to the mustache, twisting it. Eyes dart. He won't admit it verbally.
CuriousOne eyebrow up. Head tilted down, looking over an imaginary pair of glasses. Suspicious curiosity.

5. Character 03 — The Inventor Boy Proposed

A. Concept & Cast Contribution

Why this character belongs in the cast: The girl is brave but private, the baker is warm but guarded. The cast needs a character who is openly enthusiastic, socially generous, and physically comic—someone who creates momentum. The inventor boy provides energy, humor, and a reason for stories to happen: his inventions go wrong, his curiosity drags people into situations, and his good intentions create chaos that reveals other characters' true natures.

What he adds that no one else does: Physical comedy through clumsiness. A reason for mechanical/technological plot devices within the handcrafted world. A foil for the girl's caution (he acts before thinking; she thinks before acting). The friendship dynamic the audience wants to root for.

Risk if removed: The cast becomes too interior and slow. Without an external energy source, stories rely entirely on the girl's internal journey and the baker's emotional walls, which limits the range of episodes and tones available.

B. Identity

Category
Major Supporting / Best Friend
Age Impression
10–12 years old
Emotional Core
He believes everything can be fixed, built, or improved. His optimism is genuine and tested—things fail constantly, and he keeps going.
Personality
Enthusiastic, clumsy, loyal, talkative, oblivious to social cues, surprisingly sensitive
Greatest Strength
He never gives up on an idea or a friend.
Main Weakness
He doesn't listen. He's already building the solution before anyone finishes describing the problem.
Core Contradiction
He builds things to bring people together but is so absorbed in building that he forgets to be present.

C. Naming Proposals

Pim Recommended
Dutch/Nordic feel. Very short, slightly comical, easy to shout across a village square. The percussive "P" suits his energetic personality. It sounds like a small, fast sound—a spring popping, a bolt dropping—which matches his inventor identity.
Oskar
Germanic. Has more weight and seriousness than Pim, which could create an interesting contrast with his goofy personality. Feels like a name his parents gave him hoping he'd be dignified.
Fenn
Short, unusual, nature-adjacent (fen = wetland). Slightly mysterious for a character who is not mysterious at all, which is charming.

D. Physical Design (Proposed — Inspired by Reference)

Height
~4.5 heads tall. Taller than the girl but much thinner.
Build
Lanky. Long arms and legs. Knobby joints. All elbows and knees. Looks like he grew three inches overnight and his body hasn't adjusted.
Head
Slightly elongated vertically. Large ears that stick out. Narrow chin.
Hair
Brown, wild, unkempt. Sticks up in every direction. There's always something in it (a pencil, a wire, sawdust).
Eyes
Large behind oversized round glasses. Magnified, making them look even bigger. Bright, constantly moving.
Glasses
Thick round frames, slightly too big. Dark brown or black. Held together with tape or wire at the bridge. One lens slightly different from the other (a replacement).
Torso
Mustard/ochre knit sweater (hand-knit texture visible). Denim overalls over it, loose and functional with many pockets.
Hands
Disproportionately large. Stained with ink and grease. Fidgeting constantly.
Feet
Oversized brown boots, unlaced or loosely laced. Mismatched striped socks visible above.
Accessories
A leather satchel/toolbox slung across his body, full of copper tubes, gears, small tools, rolled papers.

6. Character 04 — The Flower Grandmother Proposed

A. Concept & Cast Contribution

Why this character belongs in the cast: The cast currently trends young and active. The grandmother provides counterweight: stillness, patience, observation. She is the character who sees what others miss. In story terms, she is the bridge between the mundane village and its mysterious edges—she knows things, but reveals them only through indirect hints, gifts of specific flowers, and well-timed silences.

What she adds that no one else does: Access to the village's history and its deeper mysteries. A different kind of warmth than the baker's (hers is offered freely, without resistance). A tonal anchor for quieter, more contemplative episodes. The only character who might genuinely understand the subtle strangeness at the world's edges.

Risk if removed: The mystery dimension of the world has no human face. The village lacks intergenerational depth. The girl has no female mentor figure, and the cast becomes an all-male supporting ensemble around a single girl protagonist.

B. Identity

Category
Supporting / Mentor / Mystery
Age Impression
70–80 years old
Emotional Core
She has already lived the adventures the children are beginning. She carries both the joy and the loss of that experience, and she chooses to share the joy.
Personality
Gentle, amused, perceptive, private, unhurried, quietly mischievous
Greatest Strength
She listens. Really listens. And she answers with exactly what someone needs, even if it's not what they asked for.
Main Weakness
She keeps secrets. Some of them might be better shared. Her protectiveness can feel like deception.
Core Contradiction
She is the most open and welcoming character, yet she is the one hiding the most.

C. Naming Proposals

Baba Vela Recommended
"Baba" is the universal grandmother—used across Eastern European, Central Asian, and Middle Eastern cultures. "Vela" evokes both "veil" (she hides things) and the Latin for "sails" (she navigates unseen currents). The village would call her Baba Vela as a title of warm respect. Children would just say "Baba."
Nana Suri
"Suri" means "rose" in some traditions, connecting to her garden. "Nana" is a warm, familiar grandmother title. Slightly more international-feeling.
Mirja
Finnish form of Miriam. Quiet, old-fashioned, and rare outside the north. A formal name the village would soften to "Miri" or just "Grandmother."

D. Physical Design (Proposed — Inspired by Reference)

Height
~3 heads tall. The smallest character. Slightly hunched forward.
Build
Round, soft, compact. No sharp angles anywhere. She is made of circles.
Head
Round. Prominent cheekbones under soft, lined skin. Small chin. A face shaped by decades of smiling.
Hair
Silver-white, wild curly, voluminous. Untameable. Flyaways catch the light like a halo.
Eyes
Small but very alive. Bright. Crinkled at the corners. She's always slightly amused.
Nose
Round, rosy, prominent. Pink-tipped. Warm and friendly.
Mouth
Natural resting expression is a gentle, closed-mouth smile. Deep smile lines.
Torso
Lavender hand-knit cardigan over a floral-print dress. Layers. A small apron. Everything slightly mismatched in a way that feels intentional and artistic.
Feet
Worn brown leather boots, practical, slightly too big. She shuffles slightly when she walks.
Signature Object
A large wicker basket, always overflowing with wildflowers, herbs, or occasionally something unexpected (a book, a strange stone, a bird's nest).

7. Cast Relationships

The Girl ↔ The Baker — The emotional core. She appears at his bakery uninvited. He pretends to be annoyed. He leaves bread where she'll find it. Neither acknowledges what's happening. This relationship should make viewers feel something before either character says a word about it.
The Girl ↔ The Inventor Boy — An unlikely friendship. She is cautious; he is reckless. She grounds him; he pulls her out of her shell. Their friendship should feel earned, not assumed—developed through shared experience, not instant bonding.
The Girl ↔ Baba Vela — The grandmother sees something in the girl that the girl doesn't see in herself. She gives her things (a flower, a word, a direction) that only make sense later. There is a deeper connection hinted at but never fully explained early on.
The Baker ↔ Baba Vela — They have history. Something happened years ago. They are cordial but there's a tension neither will explain. Baba Vela brings him flowers; he pretends not to notice. He sends bread to her cottage through the boy, never directly.
The Inventor Boy ↔ The Baker — The baker tolerates the boy because the boy is useful (he fixes things). The boy is terrified of the baker but fascinated by his oven. Comic dynamic. The boy is the one who delivers bread to Baba Vela, which means he's the unknowing messenger between two people who won't speak directly.
The Inventor Boy ↔ Baba Vela — She gives him strange objects and says "you'll know what to do with this." He doesn't, until three episodes later when he does. She is the only person who never criticizes his inventions.

8. Production Pipeline

The approved workflow for developing these characters into production-ready Blender assets.

1
Character Identity Documents Draft v0.2
Full personality, design, naming, relationship documentation for all cast members.
2
Executive Producer Review Awaiting
You approve names, relationships, world concept, art direction, and proposed characters before any modeling begins.
3
Blender Blockout — Girl & Baker
Primary form blockouts using approved references. Proportion verification. Silhouette tests.
4
Geometry Refinement
Secondary forms, facial topology, deformation-ready edge flow. Milestone renders for approval.
5
Materials & Textures
Full material development following the art direction material language. Shader documentation.
6
Facial System
Shape keys or bone-based expressions. Eight required expressions per character. Asymmetry tests.
7
Rigging
Character-appropriate skeletons. IK/FK where needed. Deformation testing. Animator-friendly controls.
8
Animation Readiness Tests
Silhouette, expression, deformation, and performance tests per the studio checklist, plus a 9:16 phone-size readability test.
9
Audit Submission
Complete character package submitted to Session 03 (Audit Studio) for independent review.
10
Character Release
Final approved assets packaged and delivered to Session 02 (Animation Studio) for production.
