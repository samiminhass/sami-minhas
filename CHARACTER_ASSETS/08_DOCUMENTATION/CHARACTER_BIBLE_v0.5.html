1. World Concept

Kestrava — A Village Between Seasons Approved

A small, self-contained village nestled in a valley between low, rounded hills and dense forests. The architecture is hand-built: thick stone walls, timber frames, clay-tile roofs weathered to irregular earthy tones. Streets are narrow, paved in uneven cobblestone. Every surface carries the patina of generations—worn doorsteps, faded painted signs, iron hinges green with age.

Key Locations

The Bakery
Heart of the village. Warm light spills from small windows. Flour dust hangs in the air. The oven is enormous and ancient, built into the stone wall itself.
The Square
A small irregular plaza with a stone fountain (dry in summer, overflowing in spring). Market stalls appear on certain days. Pigeons and stray cats.
The Old Bridge
A single stone arch over a narrow stream. Moss-covered, slightly crooked. The place where children gather and secrets are told.
The Meadow
Beyond the last houses. Wildflowers in every season. Rolling hills, scattered trees, a view of distant mountains. Where the girl goes to think.
The Workshop Shed
A leaning timber outbuilding behind someone's garden. Full of junk, gears, half-built contraptions, and the smell of machine oil and sawdust.
The Grandmother's Cottage
Slightly apart from the village, surrounded by an overgrown garden that seems to bloom regardless of season. Herbs hang from the ceiling inside.
The Forest Edge
Where the known world ends. Dense, old-growth trees. Strange sounds. Paths that seem to shift. Nobody goes far in.
The Cellar Market
Beneath the square. Vaulted stone ceilings, lantern-lit. Where unusual things are traded, and rumors begin.

World Rules

  • The world is grounded and believable. Physics work. People get tired, hungry, wet.
  • Subtle strangeness exists at the edges: a door that wasn't there yesterday, a flower that blooms in frost, a sound from the forest with no source.
  • Magic is never explained or systematic. It is atmospheric, ambiguous, and rare.
  • Seasons change and matter. Winter means real cold. Summer means real heat. The village transforms with each season.
  • Animals behave like animals. They are not comic sidekicks. (A cat is a cat.)
  • The village has a past. There are things the adults don't talk about. Old photographs. A locked room. An abandoned building.

Era and Technology Approved

Timeless, roughly early-to-mid twentieth century, and never dated on screen. There are no phones, screens or cars in the village. Electricity is patchy: a few bulbs and a radio, but the bakery oven burns wood and most homes use oil lamps. Machines are mechanical: clocks, bicycles, hand pumps, a mill wheel.

Why: problems get solved with hands, which keeps stories physical and easy to read without words. It gives the Inventor Boy a world of springs, gears and copper, and it keeps the series from dating. The Girl's canvas sneakers fit, because shoes like them predate this era.

Cultural Texture Approved

A fictional valley that belongs to no real country. The architecture draws on Central European and Alpine village building: steep roofs, timber balconies, thick whitewashed stone and painted shutters. Shop signs use pictures (a loaf, a key, a boot) instead of words, so no viewer meets a language barrier.

Character names should share one sound family: soft, Central European in feel, and easy to say in any language. Mila, Borek and Vela fit. Pim is Dutch and sits slightly outside it. Oskar fits better if consistency matters more than punch.

Village name: Kestrava. It replaces the working name "Millbrook", which is English and shared by many real places. Kestrava was chosen from three candidates (Kestrava, Lindvel, Ollvary) because it fits the sound family and is the most distinctive to search for. A web search found no existing place or brand with this name. This was not a trademark or social-handle check.

Dialogue Policy Approved

Silent-first for the pilot shorts. Characters vocalise (hums, grunts, gasps, a muttered syllable) but speak no words.

  • One video works on all three platforms and in every country, with no subtitles or translation.
  • The acting has to carry the story, and character acting is our second priority after story.
  • It suits the Baker, whose mouth is hidden by his mustache.
  • There is no voice-casting or voice-consistency risk at launch.

The face rigs will still include mouth shapes for speech, so dialogue remains possible for later YouTube episodes. Approved by the Executive Producer, and the Animation Studio has been informed.

Story Engine: One Central Mystery Approved

v0.3 gave four characters unexplained pasts. That is too many open questions for an audience to track across 30-second episodes. The proposal is one central question, with everything else as small reveals that can be answered.

Central question: who made the bunny? The Girl is new to the village and arrives with the bunny. Its seams use an unusual crossed stitch, the same crossed pattern as the cord binding her braid. Over time, viewers spot that stitch elsewhere in the village: a patch on the Baker's oldest apron, a cushion in a cottage window. It is a visual clue with no magic, and each sighting makes a two-second hook.

The answer is your decision. It can stay open a long time without being forgotten, because the stitch is visible in every shot she's in.

2. Art Direction

Visual Identity

Handcrafted stop-motion-inspired cinematic 3D. Every surface should look like it was made by hand—stitched, carved, sculpted, knitted, baked. The world should feel like a physical miniature set brought to life, with the precision and warmth of Laika-quality production design, but with our own proportional language, color identity, and character philosophy.

House Style Rules

What makes this look ours, beyond a general "stop-motion style":

  • The stitch is the motif. Every handmade object shows how it was made: seams, darns, bindings, patches. Thread is the series' visual signature, and it carries the central mystery.
  • Pressed, not perfect. Skin and soft props carry faint hand-sculpt marks, like pressed clay, at about 0.5–1 mm scale. They show in close-ups and disappear in wide shots.
  • Tabletop light. Lighting and focus behave like a well-lit miniature set: practical lamps in frame, gentle falloff, shallow focus in close-ups.
  • No fake frame-to-frame jitter. Simulated stop-motion surface "boil" is off by default. It distracts at phone size, and frame-to-frame noise compresses badly on platform video, causing blocky artifacts.

Colour and Value Separation

  • Environments use the desaturated, mid-value range. Characters own the most saturated colour in the frame.
  • In the references, the Girl reads as a dark top (hair) over a warm mid-value body, and the Baker reads as a light mass (jacket) with a dark accent (mustache). Keep that contrast: in a two-shot they read as opposites at a glance.
  • The Girl's coral tunic sits close to terracotta roofs and warm bakery brick. Stage her against stone, wood, sky or shadow, or separate her with a rim light.
  • The Baker's cream jacket must not sit against flour-white walls. The bakery interior uses smoke-darkened plaster and dark wood, so he stays the light shape in his own space.

Lighting Philosophy

  • Exterior: Warm golden-hour key, cool sky fill, subtle rim separation. Time-of-day shifts between scenes.
  • Interior (bakery): Warm practical lights (oven glow, window light), deep shadows, orange fill from fire.
  • Interior (cottage): Dappled light through small windows, filtered through plants and curtains. Soft, green-tinged.
  • Night: Deep blue environment, practical warm lamplight, strong contrast.

Proportional Rules (All Characters)

  • Heads are approximately 1/3 to 1/4 of total body height (large, expressive).
  • Hands are slightly oversized for emotional gesturing.
  • Feet are grounded and substantial—big enough to convey weight.
  • Eyes are the dominant facial feature. Eye size scales with emotional openness of the character.
  • Proportions express personality: round = warm/safe, angular = sharp/guarded, tall/thin = nervous/energetic.

Vertical & Mobile Readability

Our first releases are 15–45 second vertical shorts watched on phones. Every character design must hold up at that size. Under Directive Addendum 01 this is a formal Character Lab responsibility: characters, expressions and assets must read clearly in vertical mobile video.

The three duration tiers ask different things of the characters. A 15-second short is often a single reaction, so one expression has to land almost instantly. Longer 25–45 second shorts lean on expression changes and body language over several beats, so transitions between expressions must read as clearly as the expressions themselves.

  • Expressions at phone size: each of the eight expressions must be identifiable in a 9:16 medium shot viewed at 360 × 640 px. If a tester can't name the emotion in about one second, the expression needs a stronger shape change, not more detail.
  • Eye contrast: eyes need a clear value step between white, iris and skin, plus a dependable catchlight. In short-form, the eyes carry most reactions.
  • Silhouette in a tall frame: each signature shape (the Girl's braid and bunny, the Baker's width and mustache) must read when the character fills about half the frame height.
  • Two-shots: 9:16 leaves little width. The Girl–Baker size ratio should support staging in depth, with one character nearer the camera, instead of side by side. We will check this with a blockout two-shot before locking proportions.
  • Dark values: the Girl's black hair and the Baker's dark mustache and brows need rim or edge separation in every lighting setup. Without it they merge into dark backgrounds and lose shape in compressed mobile video.

Known risk: the Baker's eyes. In the reference they are small and shadowed by his brows. That suits his personality but may disappear at phone size. Proposed handling, without redesigning him: keep the eye size, add a dependable catchlight, and give the brows enough lift to reveal the eyes on key beats. The mustache and brows stay his main expression carriers. We will test this at blockout and report back before changing anything visible.

A. Identity

Category
Protagonist / Lead
Age Impression
8–10 years old
Role
Central character. The audience's entry point into Millbrook.
Emotional Core
Fierce tenderness. She protects what she loves with everything she has, even when she's scared.
Personality
Brave, curious, stubborn, imaginative, private, loyal
Greatest Strength
She never abandons someone or something she cares about.
Main Weakness
She doesn't easily trust new people. She holds on too tight—to her bunny, to her routines, to the way things were.
Core Contradiction
She craves adventure but is afraid of losing what she already has.
What Makes Her Interesting
She looks like she's been through something. There's a visible history in her worn clothes, her fierce grip on the bunny, her wariness. But she hasn't become hard—she's still open enough to be surprised.

Working Backstory Approved with the world

Situation
She has just arrived in the village to live with a relative. Where she came from, and why, is not explained early. The audience learns the village with her.
Want
To go back to how things were.
Need
To let new people matter to her.
Why the bakery
It's warm, it smells like somewhere she used to know, and nobody there asks her questions.
The braid binding
Design reason: the crossed cord was tied by whoever made the bunny, and she won't let anyone redo it. Optional long-run device: the cord frays slowly across episodes, so the day someone re-ties it becomes an emotional payoff. This adds continuity tracking and asset variants, so it needs your approval.

B. Naming Proposals

Mila Approved
Slavic origin, means "gracious" or "dear." Short, warm, easy to say in any language. Feels like a real village child's name, not a fantasy invention. The soft "m" opening and the open "a" ending carry warmth, but the name is also crisp enough for a character who can be sharp. Works naturally in dialogue: "Mila, come inside." "Mila found it." "That's Mila's."
Luma
Latin root for "light." Slightly more unusual, slightly more lyrical. Evokes the warm glow of the village. Could feel too precious if the character needs to also feel scrappy and rough.
Wren
English, after the small determined bird. Strong consonant, one syllable, feels gutsy and compact. Matches her scrappy energy. Risk: might feel more Anglo/modern than the European village setting.
Runa
Norse origin, meaning "secret" or "hidden lore." Connects to the mysterious edges of the world. A name that carries story within it—why was she named this?

4. Character 02 — Borek Approved

A. Identity

Category
Major Supporting / Potential Deuteragonist
Age Impression
50–60 years old
Role
The village's anchor. His bakery is where people come, whether they need bread or not. His relationship with the girl is the emotional spine of the series.
Emotional Core
A man who lost something important and replaced the absence with routine, discipline, and bread. His gruffness is armor. The girl is slowly finding a crack in it.
Personality
Gruff, proud, territorial, ritualistic, surprisingly perceptive, quietly generous
Greatest Strength
He makes everyone feel fed—literally and emotionally—without admitting he cares.
Main Weakness
He can't say what he feels. He substitutes action for words, bread for affection, grumbling for concern.
Core Contradiction
He pushes people away with his manner and draws them in with his actions.
What Makes Him Interesting
His design is comic (the mustache, the belly, the arms-crossed stance) but his emotional truth is deeply sympathetic. He is simultaneously the funniest and the saddest character.

Sharpening the Archetype Proposal

"Grumpy baker with a soft heart" is a familiar type, and a curled mustache on a baker can tip into a national caricature. These proposals make him specific:

His confidant
He talks only to his sourdough starter, kept in a cracked jar he has fed every morning for decades. It mirrors the Girl and her bunny: two characters who confide in an object instead of a person. The audience sees the parallel before either of them does.
His refusal
He is the best bread baker in the valley and has not baked anything sweet in years. He won't say why. The day he bakes something sweet is a payoff the series can earn slowly.
His comedy
Stillness. He wins arguments by not moving. The mustache betrays what his face won't show, and his clogs announce him before he's on screen.
Avoiding caricature
No accent gags and no national costume signifiers (toque, neckerchief, striped shirt). The mustache is his personal grooming ritual, not a nationality.

B. Naming Proposals

Borek Approved
Central/Eastern European feel. The hard "B" and the rolling "r" match his physical presence. Also subtly evokes "borek," a baked pastry—a quiet joke embedded in the name. Sounds like someone who would fold his arms and say nothing. Easy for children to say and remember.
Gustl
Bavarian diminutive of Gustav. Affectionate despite the character's gruffness—suggests someone the village has known since he was a boy. Warm, familiar, slightly comic.
Hamish
Scottish Gaelic form of James. Sounds sturdy, heavy, dependable. Matches the barrel-shaped physique. Risk: might locate the world too specifically in Scotland.
Tadek
Polish diminutive of Tadeusz. Warm, compact, grandfatherly. Has a gentle ending that contrasts with his tough exterior.

Barrel-shaped torso, massive forearms, short thick legs. His mass is in his upper body. Center of gravity is high and forward.

Facial Hair
Signature feature: thick, dark brown handlebar mustache, waxed into dramatic upward curls. The mustache is his pride. It moves independently as a secondary animation feature.
Torso
Cream/off-white baker's jacket. Flour-dusted. Wooden buttons. Slightly too tight across the belly. Linen apron tied over it, stained with flour and dough.

Feet
Wooden clogs. Simple, practical, loud on cobblestone. Flour-dusted.
Arms
Forearms are exposed and hairy. Thick wrists. Hands are enormous—kneading hands. Often crossed over his chest.
Signature Object
A round bread loaf, often carried under one arm or held out to someone (the only way he says "I care").

5. Character 03 — Pim, the Inventor Boy Proposed character

A. Concept & Cast Contribution

Why this character belongs in the cast: The girl is brave but private, the baker is warm but guarded. The cast needs a character who is openly enthusiastic, socially generous, and physically comic—someone who creates momentum. The inventor boy provides energy, humor, and a reason for stories to happen: his inventions go wrong, his curiosity drags people into situations, and his good intentions create chaos that reveals other characters' true natures.

What he adds that no one else does: Physical comedy through clumsiness. A reason for mechanical/technological plot devices within the handcrafted world. A foil for the girl's caution (he acts before thinking; she thinks before acting). The friendship dynamic the audience wants to root for.

Risk if removed: The cast becomes too interior and slow. Without an external energy source, stories rely entirely on the girl's internal journey and the baker's emotional walls, which limits the range of episodes and tones available.

Three Directions

6. Character 04 — Vela, the Flower Grandmother Proposed character

A. Concept & Cast Contribution

Why this character belongs in the cast: The cast currently trends young and active. The grandmother provides counterweight: stillness, patience, observation. She is the character who sees what others miss. In story terms, she is the bridge between the mundane village and its mysterious edges—she knows things, but reveals them only through indirect hints, gifts of specific flowers, and well-timed silences.

What she adds that no one else does: Access to the village's history and its deeper mysteries. A different kind of warmth than the baker's (hers is offered freely, without resistance). A tonal anchor for quieter, more contemplative episodes. The only character who might genuinely understand the subtle strangeness at the world's edges.

Risk if removed: The mystery dimension of the world has no human face. The village lacks intergenerational depth. The girl has no female mentor figure, and the cast becomes an all-male supporting ensemble around a single girl protagonist.

Baba Vela Approved as "Vela"
"Baba" is the universal grandmother—used across Eastern European, Central Asian, and Middle Eastern cultures. "Vela" evokes both "veil" (she hides things) and the Latin for "sails" (she navigates unseen currents). The village would call her Baba Vela as a title of warm respect. Children would just say "Baba." v0.4 note: "Baba" also calls up Baba Yaga, the witch of Slavic folklore. Combined with a forest-edge cottage and secret knowledge, it pushes her toward a witch reading. See the three directions below.
Nana Suri
"Suri" means "rose" in some traditions, connecting to her garden. "Nana" is a warm, familiar grandmother title. Slightly more international-feeling.
Mirja
Finnish form of Miriam. Quiet, old-fashioned, and rare outside the north. A formal name the village would soften to "Miri" or just "Grandmother."

7. Cast Relationships Approved

Relationships involving Pim and Vela take effect once those characters are finalized.

Mila ↔ Borek — The emotional core. She appears at his bakery uninvited. He pretends to be annoyed. He leaves bread where she'll find it. Neither acknowledges what's happening. This relationship should make viewers feel something before either character says a word about it.
Mila ↔ Pim — An unlikely friendship. She is cautious; he is reckless. She grounds him; he pulls her out of her shell. Their friendship should feel earned, not assumed—developed through shared experience, not instant bonding.
Mila ↔ Vela — The grandmother sees something in the girl that the girl doesn't see in herself. She gives her things (a flower, a word, a direction) that only make sense later. There is a deeper connection hinted at but never fully explained early on.
Borek ↔ Vela — They have history. Something happened years ago. They are cordial but there's a tension neither will explain. Vela brings him flowers; he pretends not to notice. He sends bread to her cottage through the boy, never directly. v0.4: this shared past should become one answerable reveal, not a second open mystery (see Story Engine).
Pim ↔ Borek — The baker tolerates the boy because the boy is useful (he fixes things). The boy is terrified of the baker but fascinated by his oven. Comic dynamic. The boy is the one who delivers bread to Baba Vela, which means he's the unknowing messenger between two people who won't speak directly.
Pim ↔ Vela — She gives him strange objects and says "you'll know what to do with this." He doesn't, until three episodes later when he does. She is the only person who never criticizes his inventions.

8. Production Specifications

Build targets for the Girl (CHR01) and the Baker (CHR02). Everything here is provisional until the blockout stage confirms it. Any value that changes will be updated here and noted in the change history.

A. Scale and Orientation

Units
Metric, 1 Blender unit = 1 m. Real-world scale keeps lights, subsurface, depth of field and physics behaving predictably.
Orientation
Z-up. Character faces −Y (Blender front view). Origin on the floor, centred between the feet.
Girl height
≈ 1.15 m at about 4 heads, giving a head height of about 0.29 m.
Baker height
≈ 1.55 m at about 3.5 heads, giving a head height of about 0.44 m. Widest point, with arms crossed, is about 0.75 × his height.
Height ratio
Girl ≈ 0.74 × Baker. To be confirmed with a 9:16 two-shot blockout that tests depth staging.

C. Topology Requirements

  • All deforming meshes are quads only. Triangles and n-gons are allowed only on props that never deform.
  • Eyes: at least 3 concentric loops around each eye socket. Eyeballs are separate spheres with a modelled cornea bulge and iris dish so catchlights read.
  • Mouth: at least 4 concentric loops. On the Baker these continue under the mustache so the hidden mouth still pushes the mustache and cheeks.
  • Joints: 3 supporting loops at elbows, knees, wrists and knuckles, plus an extra shoulder loop so raised arms don't collapse.
  • Baker belly: radial loop flow centred on the navel, ready for a jiggle bone.
  • Model symmetrically on X. Asymmetry lives only in shape keys, textures and posing.
  • Before handoff: consistent outward normals, no non-manifold edges on deforming meshes, no interior faces, and no unapplied scale.

F. Rig Specification

Body
Rigify generated from a metarig adapted to each character's proportions. IK/FK switching on arms and legs, FK spine with IK option, root and global controls.
Face
Shape keys driven by on-face bone controls, built as combinable components (brow raise, lower and pinch L/R, lid close L/R, smile and frown L/R, jaw open, and so on) rather than eight baked poses. Eye aim target with a per-eye offset.
Girl extras
Braid chain of 6–8 bones, FK with optional baked spring dynamics. Hair clump controls for flyaway masses. Bunny attachment that switches between left hand, right hand and world.
Bunny
Ear chains (3 bones each), head and limb bones for squish and hold poses. No face rig, because it stays an ordinary toy.
Baker extras
Mustache L/R chains (3 bones each) for curl, droop and bristle. Brow mass controls large enough to reveal or hide the eyes. Belly jiggle bone. Apron controls, or a baked cloth pass. Bread loaf with the same hand/world attachment switch.
Deformation tests
Arms raised overhead, deep knee bend, torso twist and bend, head turns of 70° or more, crossed arms (the Baker's default pose), bunny hugged to chest, and all eight expressions plus one asymmetric and one extreme.

G. Render and Output

Engines
Cycles (CPU) for look development and final frames. Workbench for silhouette, grayscale and blockout checks. EEVEE runs here only through software graphics and is slow, so it is not the default preview engine.
Colour management
AgX, the Blender 5.2 default. Fixed per project and never changed mid-production.
Short-form format
1080 × 1920, 24 fps master. A clean master is kept, and platform versions are exported from it.
Animation rate
Option for the Animation Studio: animating some moments on twos (12 poses per second inside 24 fps) would strengthen the stop-motion feel. This is a creative call for that department to test.
Exports
.blend is the master. GLB and FBX are for interchange: shape keys and skinning carry over, but drivers, constraints and Rigify controls do not. Exported animation must be baked.
